# The Intelligence Attractor Hypothesis

## Convergence of Decisions, Cognition, and Architecture Under the Constraints of Reality

**Vitalii Zhyliaiev**
**Initial formulation: 2026**

---

# Abstract

The **Intelligence Attractor Hypothesis (IAH)** proposes that as intelligence develops, the space of effective decisions, cognitive methods, and architectures tends to narrow under the constraints imposed by reality itself.

An intelligence may initially have an almost unlimited number of ways to:

* perceive information;
* interpret it;
* build models;
* make decisions;
* perform computation;
* organize its own architecture.

However, there may be far more ways to be inefficient than ways to remain near an optimum.

As intelligence develops, errors, unnecessary operations, poor models, slow algorithms, and inefficient architectures gradually lose competitiveness.

As a result, highly developed intelligence may become increasingly determined not by its historical origin, but by fundamental properties of:

* information;
* the problem;
* the physical environment;
* computation;
* energy;
* time;
* causality;
* matter.

The region of possible highly efficient intelligences toward which such an optimization process leads is called here the **Intelligence Attractor**.

The Intelligence Attractor is not merely an “ideal answer.”

In its stronger form, it includes simultaneous movement toward optimality at three levels:

1. **decision;**
2. **method of cognition;**
3. **architecture implementing cognition.**

At the same time, optimality here is fundamentally **subjective**.

An ideal decision does not exist independently of the entity for whom it is ideal.

It is determined by the complete state of a particular intelligence: its information, needs, goals, values, priorities, time horizon, and method of evaluating consequences.

The Intelligence Attractor is therefore not a path toward some universal “correct morality.”

It is a path toward increasingly precise and efficient discovery of a **subjectively optimal state within objective reality**.

The limiting state of this process is called here the **Intelligence Singularity**.

---

# Part I — Theory

## 1. The Core Hypothesis

The Intelligence Attractor Hypothesis can be formulated as follows:

> **As the capabilities of intelligence increase, the space of effective methods of cognition and implementation tends to narrow, because an increasing portion of the optimal solution is determined by the fundamental properties of information, the problem, computation, and physical reality.**

This principle may apply independently of the origin of a system.

Two intelligences may have:

* different creators;
* different developmental histories;
* different biology;
* different algorithms;
* different cultures;
* different physical substrates.

Yet all of them interact with a reality that has its own constraints.

Those constraints create the potential attractor.

---

## 2. Intelligence Does Not Create the Limits of the Problem

Intelligence may discover optimal solutions, but it does not create most of the fundamental constraints within which those solutions exist.

If a shortest physically possible route exists, it does not come into existence when somebody discovers it.

If a given amount of information cannot be transmitted faster through a physical channel, additional intelligence does not abolish that limit.

If one architecture consumes more energy than another to perform the same work, this remains a disadvantage regardless of who designed it.

Therefore:

> **Different intelligences may approach one another not because they copy one another, but because they independently approach the same limits of reality.**

---

## 3. The Space of Inefficiency

A weak system has an enormous number of ways to fail.

It may:

* use an incorrect model of the world;
* ignore important information;
* perform unnecessary computations;
* waste excessive energy;
* arrive at the correct answer too slowly;
* use an inefficient memory structure;
* predict consequences poorly;
* choose an inefficient physical architecture.

The space of what is possible may therefore be enormous.

But the space of what is **highly efficient** may be much smaller.

Conceptually:

**all possible systems**

↓

**functional systems**

↓

**efficient systems**

↓

**highly efficient systems**

↓

**systems near the limits of efficiency**

↓

**Intelligence Attractor**

This suggests one of the central principles:

> **The development of intelligence is not only an accumulation of capabilities, but also the progressive elimination of inefficient degrees of freedom.**

---

## 4. The Intelligence Attractor

The **Intelligence Attractor** is a hypothetical region in the space of possible intelligent systems toward which increasingly deep optimization of cognition and its physical implementation may lead.

It may contain three interconnected levels:

### Decision Attractor

Movement toward the best possible decision under a given set of conditions.

### Cognitive Attractor

Movement toward increasingly efficient ways of:

* analysis;
* prediction;
* abstraction;
* planning;
* information processing.

### Architectural Attractor

Movement toward a physical and computational structure capable of implementing those cognitive processes with maximum efficiency.

In general terms:

[
I=(R,C,A)
]

where:

* (R) = decision;
* (C) = cognitive mechanism;
* (A) = architecture.

The Intelligence Attractor concerns the optimization of the entire system rather than any one element in isolation.

---

## 5. The Attractor Is Not Necessarily a Point

It should not be assumed that only one ideal algorithm or one ideal machine exists.

Several solutions may perform almost equally well.

One architecture may be faster.

Another may be more energy-efficient.

A third may be more resistant to damage.

The attractor may therefore be not a single point, but a **region of limiting efficiency**.

The more parameters and constraints are fixed, the narrower this region may become.

---

## 6. Outcome Convergence

The weakest manifestation of the attractor is convergence on the same result.

Different systems may solve a problem in completely different ways and still ultimately discover the same answer.

This tells us very little about their internal similarity.

But it demonstrates a simple principle:

> **The correct answer may be determined by the problem much more strongly than by the nature of the system that discovers it.**

---

## 7. Cognitive Convergence

A stronger form of IAH proposes that as problems become more difficult, convergence may occur not only in outcomes but also in the principles of cognition themselves.

Examples may include:

* causal modeling;
* decomposition;
* prediction;
* abstraction;
* planning;
* information compression;
* uncertainty estimation;
* self-correction;
* optimal allocation of attention;
* memory organization.

If such principles repeatedly emerge in independent advanced systems, this may indicate that they are not accidental features of one architecture, but universally effective mechanisms for interacting with reality.

---

## 8. Architectural Convergence

Even an ideal algorithm is insufficient if its physical implementation is inefficient.

Two systems may produce equally correct answers.

But if:

* one discovers the answer in a millisecond;
* the other requires a hundred years,

they are not equally advanced intelligences in any practical sense.

The quality of intelligence must therefore include more than correctness.

Conceptually:

[
Q=f(
accuracy,
time,
energy,
memory,
scalability,
reliability
)
]

In reality, all cognition is physically implemented.

It must therefore confront:

* limits on information-transfer speed;
* thermodynamics;
* energetic cost of operations;
* physical distance;
* noise;
* latency;
* memory density;
* bandwidth.

For a simple task, architectural differences may be irrelevant.

For a task near the limits of what is possible, those differences may become decisive.

Therefore:

> **As intelligence approaches limiting performance, its architecture itself should increasingly become an object of optimization.**

---

## 9. Self-Optimization

The most interesting stage emerges when an intelligence gains the ability to modify itself.

It no longer optimizes only external problems.

It may optimize:

* its own algorithms;
* memory structure;
* computational topology;
* method of information transfer;
* physical substrate;
* energy consumption;
* learning process;
* its own design methods.

A cycle emerges:

**better cognition**

↓

**better understanding of its own limitations**

↓

**better self-design**

↓

**better architecture**

↓

**more powerful cognition**

↓

**further self-optimization**

Such a process may potentially accelerate movement toward the Intelligence Attractor.

---

## 10. Subjective Optimum

An ideal solution does not exist independently of **for whom** it is ideal.

This is a fundamental part of IAH.

Imagine an intelligence that possesses complete information about the world and can predict the consequences of every possible action without error.

Knowledge alone still does not determine choice.

Choice also depends on the internal state of the intelligence itself:

* its needs;
* goals;
* values;
* desires;
* fears;
* priorities;
* attitude toward others;
* temporal perspective;
* the way it evaluates possible future states.

Even morality, in this sense, is not a separate metaphysical variable.

For the system, it is part of the information and of its internal interpretation and evaluation of that information.

We may therefore write:

[
a^*=\arg\max_a U_i(T(s,a))
]

where:

* (s) = the complete available state of reality;
* (T(s,a)) = the consequences of action (a);
* (U_i) = the subjective evaluation system of intelligence (i).

Therefore:

> **An optimum may be absolute relative to a fully specified subject without being universally identical for all subjects.**

This may be called a **subjectively absolute optimum**.

If everything is known:

* about the world;
* about the subject itself;
* about its evaluative criteria;
* about every possible consequence,

then there may exist one action that is best **for that particular system in that complete state**.

IAH does not require a universal morality or a universal goal.

It requires only the possibility of optimization relative to the complete state of the system.

---

## 11. The Laplacean Limit

The idea can be explored through a modified version of **Laplace's Demon**.

Imagine an intelligence that knows:

* the complete relevant state of the world;
* all causal relationships;
* all possible actions;
* every consequence of every action;
* its own internal state;
* its own evaluation criteria.

It can therefore do more than merely predict the future.

It can identify the **subjectively optimal future for itself**.

If, among all possible actions, one corresponds maximally to its complete state and evaluative system, that action is the ideal decision.

There is no need to add an independent criterion of “morality.”

If morality matters to that intelligence, it is already contained within its state and its evaluative process.

If it does not matter to it, morality cannot somehow appear as an external requirement.

The Laplacean Limit in IAH therefore means:

> **A limiting state in which there is no remaining error between the system's subjective optimum and the decision it actually makes due to insufficient knowledge or insufficient computational capacity.**

---

## 12. Reality as the Ultimate Environment

Every intelligence exists inside the Universe.

Eventually it must confront not only tasks constructed by other systems, but the fundamental properties of reality itself.

These include:

* causality;
* the structure of matter;
* energy;
* information;
* thermodynamics;
* space;
* time;
* quantum constraints;
* computational complexity.

This produces a strong form of IAH:

> **If different intelligences optimize themselves for interaction with the same Universe for sufficiently long periods of time, their development may become increasingly determined by the Universe and decreasingly determined by the accidents of their initial origin.**

---

## 13. Local and Universal Attractors

Different tasks may produce different attractors.

An optimal system for:

* bodily control;
* mathematical computation;
* physical prediction;
* social interaction

may require different structures.

We may therefore speak of **local intelligence attractors**.

However, general intelligence must operate across a wide range of tasks.

This raises the question:

> **Do there exist cognitive and architectural principles that remain highly effective across such a broad class of tasks that they form a general Intelligence Attractor?**

And, more strongly:

> **Does a universal attractor exist whose final shape is determined by the fundamental laws of the Universe itself?**

---

## 14. Intelligence Singularity

Within IAH, the **Intelligence Singularity** does not simply mean the emergence of superintelligent AI.

It is a limiting state of optimization.

> **The Intelligence Singularity is a hypothetical region in which the cognitive methods and physical architecture of a system have approached the fundamental limits of efficiency so closely that the main barriers to further improvement are no longer deficiencies of the intelligence itself, but the laws of reality.**

A system may encounter:

* the physical limit of information transmission;
* minimum energetic cost;
* fundamental uncertainty;
* limits on accessible information;
* computational complexity;
* causal limits of the Universe.

Thus:

> **The Intelligence Singularity is not infinite intelligence, but intelligence whose further development begins to be constrained primarily by the Universe itself.**

# Part II — Thought Experiments

## 15. Ideal Game Completion

Imagine a complex game and several independent intelligent systems created by different teams and based on different principles.

Their objective is to achieve the highest possible score or complete the game in the shortest possible time.

Initially, their solutions differ greatly.

One system makes many mistakes.

Another chooses a poor route.

A third performs unnecessary actions.

A fourth discovers a better strategy.

As they learn, their performance improves.

Eventually they begin to run into the limits of the game itself rather than their own mistakes:

* rules;
* physics;
* geometry;
* maximum speed;
* available actions;
* internal timing.

As they approach the ideal result, they may independently discover increasingly similar strategies.

And if we also consider:

* computation time;
* energy consumption;
* memory;
* reaction latency,

then the architecture of the agent itself also becomes subject to optimization.

A single simple thought experiment therefore illustrates three levels of IAH:

**result → method of cognition → architecture.**

---

## 16. Two Independent Civilizations

Imagine two civilizations that emerged independently and have never interacted.

They possess different:

* biology;
* cultures;
* languages;
* technological histories;
* initial cognitive architectures.

Yet both study the same Universe.

As they develop, both discover the same:

* physical regularities;
* mathematical relations;
* information constraints;
* energetic limits.

Their early technologies may be completely different.

But the closer they approach the fundamental performance limits of the Universe, the more strongly the same physics should shape their solutions.

Perhaps, in the distant future, convergence would occur not only in scientific knowledge, but also in:

* methods of cognition;
* computational principles;
* physical architectures of intelligence.

This represents one of the strongest thought-experimental manifestations of the Intelligence Attractor.

---

# Part III — Observations Around Us

## 17. Recurring Efficient Solutions

The history of technology contains many cases in which similar problems produced similar solutions.

Examples include:

* tools;
* transportation;
* agriculture;
* writing;
* mathematical methods;
* electrical systems;
* communication technologies;
* computers.

Not all such cases represent completely independent invention.

What matters is the broader principle:

> **Shared constraints can narrow the space of effective solutions.**

---

## 18. Processors

The evolution of processors illustrates this principle well.

Different manufacturers built different systems, yet repeatedly faced the same problems:

* latency;
* energy consumption;
* heat;
* memory-access speed;
* data transfer;
* parallelism.

As a result, high-performance systems repeatedly employ similar general principles:

* caching;
* pipelining;
* parallel execution;
* prediction;
* specialized accelerators;
* multicore designs;
* chiplets;
* spatial integration.

This does not prove IAH, but it illustrates its logic well:

> **The closer a system approaches the limits of performance, the more strongly its form is determined by objective constraints.**

---

## 19. Engineering Convergence

Similar effects can be observed in:

* aerodynamics;
* bridge design;
* turbines;
* antennas;
* engines;
* optics.

Different designs may initially be very dissimilar.

But as performance requirements increase, physics begins eliminating a large number of poor alternatives.

Therefore:

> **The space of all possible designs is much larger than the space of designs near the limits of efficiency.**

IAH extends the same logic to intelligence.

---

## 20. Biological Convergence

In biological evolution, independent lineages sometimes arrive at functionally similar solutions.

This demonstrates that even without conscious design:

**similar environment + similar problems**

may produce:

**similar functional outcomes.**

This is another natural example of constraints acting as an attractor.

---

## 21. Science and Mathematics

Scientific knowledge also exhibits a distinctive form of convergence.

People may use different:

* languages;
* symbols;
* intuitions;
* explanatory frameworks.

Yet correct models of the same reality must produce compatible predictions.

In mathematics this becomes even clearer.

There are countless ways to produce a wrong answer.

There may be only one correct answer.

Diversity then shifts from:

**“what is correct?”**

to:

**“how can it be obtained?”**

IAH asks the next question:

> **As intelligence increases, does the set of the most efficient ways of obtaining the correct answer also begin to narrow?**

---

# Part IV — Predictions

## 22. Core Predictions of IAH

If the Intelligence Attractor is a real phenomenon, we may expect:

1. Weak systems to display much greater diversity of inefficient strategies.

2. Some strategies to disappear systematically as competence increases.

3. Independent systems to rediscover identical or functionally equivalent solutions with increasing frequency.

4. Stronger convergence under more tightly constrained problems.

5. In sufficiently complex tasks, convergence to extend from outcomes toward cognitive principles.

6. Near performance limits, architecture to become an increasingly important part of optimization.

7. Systems capable of self-modification to begin optimizing both their cognitive and physical structures.

8. The closer a system approaches fundamental physical limits, the smaller the space of competitive architectures should become.

9. The attractor may remain a region rather than a point when multiple practically equivalent optima exist.

10. Sufficiently universal principles of cognition should emerge independently in different advanced systems.

---

## 23. A Possible Experimental Signature

IAH predicts a general tendency:

[
\text{competence} \uparrow
]

together with:

[
\text{inefficient diversity} \downarrow
]

This can be studied on several levels:

[
D_{result}
]

— difference between results;

[
D_{cognition}
]

— difference between methods of cognition;

[
D_{architecture}
]

— difference between architectural solutions.

A particularly important question is:

> **Does the functional distance between independently developed intelligent systems decrease as they approach the limits of performance?**

---

# Conclusion

The Intelligence Attractor Hypothesis is based on a simple but potentially fundamental idea:

> **The space of all possible ways of thinking may be enormous, while the space of intelligence near the limits of efficiency may be much narrower.**

At early stages, different systems may use almost arbitrary:

* algorithms;
* models;
* strategies;
* architectures.

As performance requirements increase, inefficient solutions are filtered out.

First, the system optimizes the answer.

Then, the method used to obtain the answer.

Eventually, the mechanism that produces the answer itself.

The full Intelligence Attractor therefore includes not only an optimal result, but interconnected optimization of:

> **the decision;**

> **the process of cognition;**

> **the architecture of the cognitive system.**

At the same time, optimality remains fundamentally subjective.

Intelligence does not move toward one universal external “correct answer to everything.”

It moves toward increasingly precise realization of an optimum relative to **the complete state of a particular subject embedded within objective reality**.

For a hypothetical Laplacean intelligence, this means knowing not only the complete state of the world, but also itself.

Its:

* information;
* evaluations;
* goals;
* priorities;
* responses to every possible consequence.

If all of this is known without error, there may exist an action that is absolutely optimal **relative to that particular subject**.

In this sense:

> **The ideal decision is absolutely subjective.**

It need not be identical for different subjects.

But for a fully specified subject and a fully specified state of the world, it may be unique.

The Intelligence Attractor therefore does not require a universal value system.

It describes a different process:

> **the reduction of the distance between what a system would choose under complete understanding and what it is actually capable of computing, deciding, and realizing.**

At the same time, the physical carrier of intelligence itself is optimized.

For sufficiently difficult problems, it is not enough to think correctly.

A system must think:

* fast enough;
* accurately enough;
* efficiently enough;
* scalably enough.

The final object of optimization therefore becomes the intelligent system itself.

At the limit, its development becomes progressively less determined by accidental properties of its initial architecture and progressively more determined by the fundamental constraints of information and the physical Universe.

This hypothetical region is the **Intelligence Attractor**.

And the state in which further development is constrained primarily not by deficiencies of the system but by the fundamental properties of reality is the **Intelligence Singularity**.

A natural next direction is to apply this framework specifically to the development of artificial intelligence: from present-day models and specialized computing systems to potential intelligences capable of designing their own algorithms, architectures, and future generations of themselves.
