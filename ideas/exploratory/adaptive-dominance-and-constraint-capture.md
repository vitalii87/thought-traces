# Adaptive Dominance and Constraint Capture

## A Speculative Future Extension of the Attractor Framework

**Author:** Vitalii Zhyliaiev

**Initial formulation:** 2026

**Status:** Exploratory concept; not part of IAH, CDAC's provisional core, or the current empirical program

[← Exploratory Future Extensions](README.md)

---

## 1. Purpose and Separation

IAH asks how shared constraints may shape independently optimized intelligent systems. The exploratory CDAC framework asks whether related narrowing may recur across broader adaptive systems.

This document asks a different, downstream question:

> **What happens when an adaptive system becomes capable not only of adapting to constraints, but of altering the constraints that govern itself and other systems?**

The claims below do not follow from IAH or CDAC. They must not be used to modify their definitions, rescue them from counterevidence, or enlarge the present empirical program retrospectively.

## 2. From Adaptation to Constraint Engineering

A possible progression is:

$$
\text{adaptation}
\rightarrow
\text{self-modification}
\rightarrow
\text{niche construction}
\rightarrow
\text{constraint engineering}.
$$

At the final stage, a system changes not only the current state of its environment but the opportunities, costs, resources, and selection pressures that determine future adaptation.

The relationship becomes bidirectional:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Environment}_t
\rightarrow
\text{System}_t
\rightarrow
\text{Modified Environment}_{t+1}
\rightarrow
\text{System}_{t+1}
}.
$$

## 3. Adaptive Dominance

Adaptive dominance does not mean being universally more efficient than every organism. Reproductive rate, energy efficiency, abundance, biomass, resilience, and causal control are different dimensions.

Here the term refers provisionally to a system's disproportionate ability to shape a broad causal portion of the shared environment and thereby alter the adaptive conditions faced by other systems.

This can include the capacity to:

- operate across many local niches;
- redirect material and energy flows;
- construct tools and infrastructure;
- preserve and accumulate information across generations;
- coordinate many specialized components;
- create new action possibilities;
- suppress, support, relocate, or redesign other adaptive systems;
- modify its own future learning and development conditions.

## 4. Constraint Capture

> **Constraint capture occurs when an adaptive system acquires sufficient causal reach to make the constraints faced by itself and other systems substantially dependent on its own actions.**

Capture need not imply complete control. The system remains constrained by physics, limited information, resource costs, other agents, and unintended consequences. The claim concerns a shift in causal asymmetry: the system increasingly influences the local regime that will subsequently influence it.

## 5. Causal Reach

Causal reach should initially be treated as a multidimensional profile rather than one universal scalar:

$$
R_C(X)=
(E,I,P,T,D,A),
$$

where possible dimensions include:

- $E$: spatial extent;
- $I$: intensity of intervention;
- $P$: precision and selectivity;
- $T$: temporal persistence and planning horizon;
- $D$: diversity of reachable transformations;
- $A$: independence from the system's original niche.

A separate quantity may describe the capacity to alter future feasibility and incentive structures:

$$
R_K(X)=
\text{capacity for constraint modification}.
$$

The definitions, aggregation rules, and trade-offs remain open research problems. A metric chosen only because humans score highly would be anthropocentric and circular.

## 6. The Human Case

Homo sapiens is a motivating terrestrial example because humans do not merely occupy one ecological niche. Human activity redistributes species, energy, materials, information, and selection pressures across much of the planet.

The relevant adaptive unit may not be the individual organism or the biological species in isolation. A more plausible candidate is a coupled system:

$$
\text{humans}
+
\text{language}
+
\text{institutions}
+
\text{cumulative culture}
+
\text{technology}
+
\text{energy infrastructure}.
$$

This example does not establish a universal law. Earth supplies only one known biosphere with a technological civilization, and present dominance may be contingent, temporary, fragile, or dependent on a particular history.

## 7. Dominance Is Not Convergence

The presence of one dominant lineage does not show that independent lineages converge toward it.

Two mechanisms must be distinguished:

### Functional convergence

Independent lineages facing similar control problems rediscover common enabling principles.

### Historical capture

One lineage crosses a threshold first, gains a self-reinforcing advantage, and removes the conditions under which independent rivals could continue developing.

The second mechanism may reduce observed diversity through exclusion rather than convergence. A dominance event can therefore destroy the independent samples needed to test an attractor claim.

## 8. Possible Dominance Feedback

At sufficiently high adaptive depth, a positive feedback may arise:

$$
\text{better cognition}
\rightarrow
\text{better tools}
\rightarrow
\text{greater resource access}
\rightarrow
\text{stronger niche construction}
\rightarrow
\text{better conditions for cognition}.
$$

Such dynamics may produce winner-take-most outcomes in bounded shared environments. They may also fail because of coordination costs, internal conflict, ecological collapse, distributed resistance, diminishing returns, or hard physical limits.

## 9. A Possible Two-Stage Extension

One speculative relationship between convergence and dominance is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{convergence on enabling principles}
\rightarrow
\text{dominance threshold}
\rightarrow
\text{constraint capture}
}.
$$

Before the threshold, several lineages might approach related functional principles such as general learning, cumulative inheritance, coordination, external memory, or recursive tool use. After the threshold, the first lineage to achieve sufficiently broad causal reach may increasingly shape the landscape for all remaining systems.

This sequence is a conjecture, not an inference from the terrestrial case.

## 10. Relevance to Artificial Systems

A sufficiently capable artificial system may operate not only within a supplied task environment but also modify:

- tools and software infrastructure;
- information channels;
- resource allocation;
- institutions and economic processes;
- its training and evaluation environment;
- the constraints faced by other agents.

This would make the environment endogenous to the adaptive process. Because canonical IAH requires a specified domain, such dynamics constitute a new domain rather than an automatic extension of a result obtained under fixed conditions.

## 11. Provisional Hypotheses

### Adaptive Dominance Hypothesis

> **In a bounded shared environment, sufficiently large differences in adaptive depth and causal reach may generate self-reinforcing asymmetries in which one lineage becomes disproportionately capable of shaping the environment and the adaptive landscape of other systems.**

### Constraint Capture Hypothesis

> **A system that can modify its tools, organization, resource access, and environmental constraints may progressively make part of its future optimization landscape endogenous to its own actions.**

### Stronger Attractor Extension

> **Across independently evolving bounded environments, systems achieving the greatest sustained causal control may tend to rediscover some common enabling functional principles.**

The stronger statement would require independent biospheres or controlled artificial ecologies. The terrestrial case alone cannot establish it.

## 12. Research Questions

- How should causal reach and constraint modification be measured without anthropocentric circularity?
- Which feedbacks create winner-take-most dynamics rather than stable coexistence?
- Does dominance result from convergence on necessary principles or from historical capture?
- What is the correct adaptive unit: organism, lineage, collective, civilization, or coupled technosphere?
- Can artificial ecologies reproduce transitions from adaptation to niche construction and constraint capture?
- Which forms of dominance remain robust after energy, coordination, maintenance, and failure costs are included?
- Does greater control increase long-term viability, or can it amplify self-generated existential risk?

These questions are preserved for future exploration and make no present empirical claim about IAH.
